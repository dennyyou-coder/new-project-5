Roborock has sent its robots into the swimming pool.

At IFA in Berlin in September 2026, the company unveiled its first pool-cleaning robot, the RockAqua P1. It had already entered robotic lawn mowing. With the P1, another significant category has joined its outdoor business. [Roborock at IFA](https://global.roborock.com/pages/ifa-2026)

I think Roborock may be the most formidable competitor the pool-robot industry has yet attracted. Judging by its first product and the pace of investment, however, it has not launched its full push into this market.

First, the product.

According to Roborock, the P1 can clean shallow platforms in as little as 15 cm of water, using sensors to detect the platform edge. That addresses a practical problem: the main pool floor may be clean while shallow steps and sun shelves still need attention by hand.

The P1 also uses vision to recognize obstacles such as drain covers. Its ClearVision patrol cleaning looks for leaves and prioritizes areas with more debris. When cleaning is complete, or the battery falls below 15%, it returns to the waterline for easier retrieval. The product page has yet to disclose full pricing and availability. [RockAqua P1 product details](https://garden.roborock.com/eu/products/roborock-rockaqua-p1)

![Official RockAqua P1 image with its published shallow-water, vision and waterline-parking features, alongside Denny's assessment that it has not yet surpassed Beatbot or Aiper.](/images/articles/roborock-rockaqua-p1-pool-robot/02-product.webp)

Based on what has been announced, the P1 looks solid but unexceptional. I do not yet see it surpassing products from Beatbot or Aiper. Shallow-water cleaning and visual recognition have practical value, but they do not make the current leaders' products look outdated.

Roborock has also stopped short of the three separate product lines it introduced when entering robotic mowing. Pool cleaning is starting with just the P1—a noticeably more restrained debut.

From what I understand, Roborock has established a dedicated outdoor-robotics team that operates separately. I suspect the different pace of development reflects how that team allocates its resources. Even with Roborock behind it, a new team can only advance so many projects at once. Robotic mowing, the larger market, would naturally receive priority.

Supply-chain sources tell me Roborock is preparing mower production and inventory at a rate of 30,000 units a month. That is a stocking and production-preparation rate, not confirmed retail sales, but it indicates that the company is preparing to scale its mower business.

At that stage, a team has to devote substantial attention to delivery and problems encountered in actual use. Performance across different gardens needs continual improvement as sales grow. Completing the first pool robot does not mean the pool business can immediately receive the same level of resources.

That is why I think Roborock's serious push into pool cleaning may not come until 2027 at the earliest. This year's P1 looks more like an initial market entry, collecting the first round of customer feedback. Once mowing operations settle down and the outdoor team has more capacity, the pool lineup could expand faster.

![Roborock outdoor-business timeline separating the current mower stocking rate of 30,000 units per month, Denny's 2027 pool-expansion forecast and the reported RMB 100 billion revenue ambition around 2030. Neither stocking nor a target is shown as realized sales.](/images/articles/roborock-rockaqua-p1-pool-robot/03-timing.webp)

Its restraint today should not be mistaken for a lack of competitive strength.

In the first half of 2026, Roborock generated RMB 10.084 billion in revenue, up 27.6%, and RMB 986 million in net profit attributable to shareholders, up 45.6%. At the end of June, cash and cash equivalents stood at approximately RMB 2.404 billion, alongside other financial assets. Earnings from its established business mean the pool project does not have to support the company from day one. [Roborock's 2026 interim report](https://money.finance.sina.com.cn/corp/view/vCB_AllBulletinDetail.php?id=12524142&stockid=688169)

From what I understand, Roborock is targeting revenue of RMB 100 billion around 2030. Reaching that scale requires substantial businesses beyond robot vacuums. Outdoor robotics is clearly an important part of that ambition.

Knowing Roborock's approach, once it commits to a category, it will aim to become one of the leaders. Giving mowers priority looks like a question of sequencing investment. As the outdoor business matures, pool robots will face higher growth expectations too.

![Roborock's first-half 2026 financial capacity: revenue of RMB 10.084 billion, attributable net profit of RMB 986 million, and cash and cash equivalents of approximately RMB 2.404 billion at June 30. These are company-wide figures, not pool-robot revenue or a project budget.](/images/articles/roborock-rockaqua-p1-pool-robot/04-financial-capacity.webp)

That distinguishes Roborock from many new entrants. For a startup, a weak first product can jeopardize the next funding round. Roborock has far more room for trial and error, and a clear reason to keep developing the business.

Pool robots particularly need that time. A machine that runs smoothly in a test pool will not necessarily handle every situation in a customer's backyard. Some problems emerge only over a full selling season; the revised product may not face its next real test until the following year. A company short of cash may not be able to wait.

Roborock has already been through years of competition in robot vacuums. It understands how much work lies between a product launch and dependable sales. With continued investment, shortcomings in the first generation can be addressed.

Its established international brand will help as well. Someone who owns a Roborock vacuum and likes it may be more receptive to a pool cleaner from the same company. A new brand has to explain who it is; Roborock can start with trust it has already earned.

But a robot-vacuum sales network does not automatically become a pool-equipment channel. Specialist pool dealers need to know how a machine will be repaired and what return they can make by selling it. Roborock still has to do that work.

Beatbot and Aiper's lead is rooted in work they have already done. They reached pool owners earlier, have products tested through actual selling seasons, and know more directly which features customers will pay for. The P1, as it stands, has not demonstrated an advantage sufficient to erase that experience.

The coming year therefore matters. If they keep improving the experience and deepen their dealer relationships, Roborock will face a harder catch-up when it increases investment. If they make only minor changes to existing products, today's distance from the P1 may not survive another generation.

In our earlier discussion of [Maytronics and its potential sale](/blog/maytronics-fimi-sale-pool-robot-leader), we examined how a traditional leader was responding to Chinese cordless pool robots, and what FIMI might change. Maytronics is still discussing an investment and control transaction with FIMI, while entrants such as Roborock are already showing products. [CTech's report](https://www.calcalistech.com/ctechnews/article/bk0011ypmdfe)

Competition will change with them. Over the past few years, Beatbot and Aiper have largely been taking business from traditional brands. Roborock's arrival will intensify competition among Chinese companies themselves. For smaller brands without much product differentiation, acquiring a customer may cost more, and staying in the market may become harder.

Roborock's first pool robot has not yet reordered the industry. But in my view, the companies already here may have only a year to prepare for its full push.
