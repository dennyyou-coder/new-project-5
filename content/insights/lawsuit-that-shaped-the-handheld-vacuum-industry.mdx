In 2018, Dyson voluntarily withdrew an appeal against SharkNinja in a case that had been brought to the courts. In the previous ruling, Judge Robert M. Dow supported SharkNinja's argument that it did not infringe on Dyson's appearance patent.

**This lawsuit may be one of the most significant design cases in the history of the vacuum cleaner industry and has implications for the entire sector.**

Let us briefly revisit two lawsuits from 2010. At that time, Dyson sued Vax in the UK over a vacuum cleaner called Mach Zen, which was part of TTI Group, and also sued Dirt Devil (also under TTI) in France regarding the same model. Both models were alleged to infringe on Dyson's design patent for its DC02 vacuum cleaner from 15 years prior. The UK court rejected Dyson’s lawsuit, while the French court sided with Dyson.

Above is a comparison of the exterior designs of two models.

Those familiar with the vacuum cleaner industry know that Dyson frequently files lawsuits over design patent infringement, and defending such lawsuits can incur significant legal fees. Even if one wins the case, the costs remain substantial.

Due to Dyson's reinvention of the cordless vacuum cleaner, which disrupted the traditional design concepts of such devices, Dyson holds numerous patents in this area. Currently, some of its domestic patents include:

> Title: Handheld cleaning appliance. Abstract: A handheld cleaning appliance (10) comprising a suction duct (14) with a longitudinal axis (Y-Y); an airflow generator (36) for producing an airflow along the suction duct; a separating apparatus (18) in communication with the suction duct for separating dirt and dust from the airflow; a power source (32) for supplying power to the airflow generator; and an elongate handle (28) located between the airflow generator and the power source. The handle is sized and positioned to be gripped by the user's hand and lies transverse to the longitudinal axis of the suction duct. This arrangement makes the handheld vacuum cleaner easier and more comfortable to manoeuvre. (Dyson Chinese patent abstract, translated)

After a period of patent awareness, many companies have learned to avoid the following areas with obvious patents:

1. Dust cup and power source placement
2. Motor and power source positioning
3. Handle angle

From Dyson's lawsuit against Eubi in 2015, these points were accepted by the court as valid patents.

> At the first-instance hearing, the court examined the product Dyson had bought from the Aibici Home website: a cordless handheld vacuum whose packaging, manual and certificate named Aibici Home as the distributor. It is a handheld cleaning appliance with a main body, a dirty-air inlet, a clean-air outlet and a cyclonic separator in the airflow path. The separator stands roughly upright and has a flat base. The handle sits between the upper part of the body, which holds the motor and fan, and the lower part, which holds the battery. The flat base of the body and the flat base of the separator together form the base of the appliance, so it can stand on a surface, with the motor, fan and battery above that base. Dyson argued the product matched every feature of claim 1. The defendant argued that because the product is used by hand at any angle, its separator is not upright and it has no base surface as described. (Chinese court judgment, translated and condensed)

> The first-instance court found that the separator is roughly perpendicular to its flat base, so it can be regarded as upright; the base of the body and the base of the separator lie in the same plane and form the base of the appliance; and the motor, fan and battery all sit above it. Even in hand, the base surface exists and the parts keep the same positions however the user turns the product. The court therefore rejected the defendant's argument and held that the product falls within the scope of claim 1. The defendant's design patent did not change this, since owning a design patent has no bearing on infringing an invention patent, and the design patent post-dated Dyson's. (Chinese court judgment, translated and condensed)

However, it is important to note that Dyson's patent refers to a "power source," which many have interpreted as a battery. This interpretation is incorrect.

In its lawsuit against Sogee in 2015, Dyson stated that the term "power source" does not exclusively refer to direct current (DC) power; alternating current (AC) power is also included within this scope.

> Dyson applied for a retrial, arguing that the second-instance judgment misread "power source" and "power cord". By reading "power cord" narrowly as a wire that carries electricity, the court excluded mains AC power from the claimed "power source", against both the skilled reader's understanding and common sense. This wrongly narrowed the patent to the dry-battery embodiment and led to the wrong comparison. Dyson asked for both earlier judgments to be overturned. (Supreme People's Court retrial ruling, translated and condensed)

In the Supreme Court's ruling on November 13, 2017, it was determined that in Dyson's patent, the power source refers only to a direct current (DC) power source, and power cords are not included.

> On the first issue, the court held that in a broad sense "power source" means a device that converts other forms of energy into electricity and covers both DC and AC sources. But the patent specification does not define it, so its meaning must be read through the specification and drawings. The specification says the handle, motor and fan assembly and power source are arranged to make the handheld vacuum easy and comfortable to manoeuvre, with the power source next to the second end of the handle so that the combined moment of the parts' centres of mass is small. That purpose is only achieved if the power source is a device with real weight. (Supreme People's Court ruling of 13 November 2017, translated and condensed)

> Paragraph [0027] of the specification also says the vacuum "need not be fitted with a rechargeable power source; standard batteries or a power cord may be used". The specification therefore distinguishes rechargeable power, standard batteries and power cords. A power cord cannot achieve the purpose of the invention, so it cannot be read into the claims, and "power source" cannot be interpreted to include a power cord. The court further found that in examining a related Dyson patent (application 200780027217.X), which shares the same priority (GB0614235.0, 18 July 2006) and essentially the same claims and description, Dyson itself stated that a power cord is not a power source. (Supreme People's Court ruling, translated and condensed)

It is clear that Dyson's defeat in the patent case hinges on Dyson's own specific statements during the substantive examination phase of the patent, where it clarified that the power cord was not a source of power. However, this ruling applies only to the domestic market; I have been informed by friends abroad that in Dyson's patents outside of China, "power" does include alternating current, meaning that the power cord is indeed included.

Now back to the main topic of this article.

On February 4, 2014, Dyson filed a patent infringement lawsuit against SharkNinja Corporation for its Rocket HV320 model in the United States (patent numbers D577,163, D668,010, and D668,823). (A comparison diagram is provided below)

![The Lawsuit That Shaped the Handheld Vacuum Industry image 10](/images/articles/lawsuit-that-shaped-the-handheld-vacuum-industry/10-image-010.webp)

Dyson is seeking $200 million in damages, making this one of only three design-infringement lawsuits with a damage claim exceeding $100 million in U.S. history.

In March 2018, Judge Robert M. Dow ruled that "even under the most favorable interpretation for Dyson, reviewing undisputed facts, the overall appearance of Shark's Rocket model is significantly different from Dyson’s patent," and thus found that Shark's Rocket does not infringe on Dyson's patent. The judge also rejected Dyson's theory that "overall similarity" could be used to support a design patent infringement claim.

This case, though not widely known, has significant implications for the industry. If Dyson's "overall appearance" theory is supported by the judge and Dyson wins against Shark, it can be anticipated that Dyson will use this theory to sue other vacuum cleaner brands. This could potentially place a large portion of cordless vacuums in infringement disputes.

As the era of cordless vacuums has arrived, how companies handle their relationship with Dyson's patent designs is becoming crucial.
