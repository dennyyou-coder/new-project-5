## What the middle Sora model adds

Sora 30 sits between the simpler Sora 10 and the surface-cleaning Sora 70. Its main ownership advantages are floating retrieval, a larger battery than Sora 10 and a four-brush underside. These features deserve separate consideration: a larger battery can extend the available cleaning window, while easier retrieval can reduce the physical effort required after every cycle.

The published 6,800 US gallons-per-hour figure is a manufacturer water-flow claim. It does not establish how much sand, algae or leaf debris the robot removes in a particular pool, and should not be compared directly with a vacuum-pressure specification from another product category.

## Floating retrieval versus surface cleaning

Sora 30 can park at the water surface so the owner can retrieve it more conveniently. That behaviour does not turn it into a skimmer. Its stated cleaning tasks remain the floor, walls and waterline; leaves floating away from the wall require another collection method.

The app also has a practical connectivity boundary. Beatbot says control is unavailable while the machine is on the floor or wall. Once it is online at the surface, the retrieval function becomes relevant. Before purchase, consider Wi-Fi coverage around the pool and where a person will stand to lift the listed 8.9 kg robot after draining.

## Check ledges and cleaning duration

The shallow-platform claim starts around 20 cm of water, with a platform at least one metre in both directions. A ledge can meet the depth figure and still present difficult transitions, curved edges or obstacles. Measure the actual pool and confirm the applicable manual rather than treating a minimum-depth headline as a guarantee for every tanning shelf.

Runtime also depends on the selected task. The official FAQ separates up to five hours of floor-only operation from up to 4.5 hours of floor, wall and waterline cleaning. Climbing and route changes consume energy differently from a largely flat-floor pass. Match the cycle to the pool’s debris pattern and inspect the result before settling on a schedule.

## Versions and market differences

The US listing showed $689 on 12 September 2026. Sora 10, Sora 30 and Sora 70 are different products, not simply interchangeable names for accessory packages. The comparison table highlights differences in battery, collection capacity and surface-cleaning capability rather than implying that a higher number improves every aspect of ownership.

For the basket, this guide follows the detailed 5.2 L specification; some advertising rounds it to 5 L. Standard filtration is 150 µm. The separately described 3 µm accessory may help target finer material, but compatibility, stock and replacement cost need checking in the relevant market. Older shipping promises on a product page are not current availability evidence.

## Maintenance and ownership

Floating retrieval reduces one part of the routine, but the owner still needs to empty and rinse the basket, inspect the brushes and tracks, and follow the charging instructions. Fine sediment can restrict a filter before a basket looks full. Cleaning frequency should reflect the debris load rather than the nominal capacity alone.

Do not infer an automatic filter-washing station from generic self-cleaning language. Sora 30 ownership still includes basket care. Beatbot’s FAQ specifies salt concentration below 5,000 ppm; check the complete water-condition requirements as well. Pool chemistry, circulation and the permanent filtration system remain separate responsibilities.

## When to choose another arrangement

Sora 10 may be sufficient when submerged cleaning is the requirement and waterline parking is manageable. Sora 70 becomes more relevant when floating debris is part of the same purchase brief. A separate iSkim can instead collect surface debris while Sora 30 handles submerged surfaces.

That two-machine arrangement adds charging, storage and maintenance considerations even if the skimmer uses solar energy. Compare it against the debris actually present: falling leaves, windblown fragments and fine floor sediment create different workloads. Select the system around those tasks rather than the assumption that every robot with floating capability performs the same cleaning job.
